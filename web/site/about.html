About Wynkyn

A typesetting tool for people who bind books by hand.

Who it is for

The hobby binder making a book or two: a family history, a favourite public-domain novel, a collection of poems for a few friends. Someone who wants the pages to look like a real book and should never have to learn CSS to get there. Every setting in Wynkyn has a plain name, like “Space between paragraphs”, and you reach it by clicking the thing on the page you want to change.

What it does

You write the manuscript in plain text with a little semantic markup for chapters, epigraphs, poems, pull quotes, sidebars, drop caps and footnotes. All styling lives apart from the words, so the manuscript stays clean and the book can be restyled without touching it. Wynkyn lays the book out in your browser with Paged.js and shows the real pages as you write: the same pagination the printer gets, so there is no surprise at the end.

For printing, it imposes the pages onto sheets as folded signatures with the right page order, duplex options for printers that cannot print both sides, and a proof mode to test one signature before you commit paper. It also writes a reflowable EPUB for readers.

Your books are yours

Wynkyn never reads, analyses, trains on, shares or sells your manuscripts, and never will. There is no server: the app is a set of plain files, and everything it does runs in your browser. In Chrome or Edge it opens a folder on your own disk and works on the files there, so a book you make in Wynkyn is a folder you can back up, version or edit with any other tool. Other browsers keep books in browser storage, and you can download a book as a zip at any time.

Free and open source

Wynkyn is MIT licensed. The source is on GitHub, where it can also run as a local app with a command-line export that renders print-ready PDFs. Everything you can do on your own device is free and will stay free.

The name

Wynkyn de Worde was William Caxton’s apprentice and, after Caxton’s death, the first printer to set up shop on Fleet Street. His shop stood at the sign of the Sun, and a sun sits in every printer’s device he used. The mark on this site is his sun.
