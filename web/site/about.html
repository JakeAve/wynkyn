About Wynkyn

A place to write, lay out and print a book you will bind by hand.

Who it is for

The hobby binder making a book or two: a family history, a favourite public-domain novel, a collection of poems for a few friends. Someone who wants the pages to look like a real book and should never have to learn CSS to get there. Every setting in Wynkyn has a plain name, like “Space between paragraphs”, and you reach it by clicking the thing on the page you want to change.

Until now the choice has been a professional layout program, which costs money every month and takes weeks to learn, or a word processor, which is easy but stops at a single page and knows nothing about signatures. Wynkyn is the tool in between: enough control to make a proper book, none of the apparatus.

What it does

You write and edit the manuscript in Wynkyn, in plain text with a few plain markers for chapters, epigraphs, poems, pull quotes, sidebars, drop caps and footnotes. All styling lives apart from the words, so the manuscript stays clean and the book can be restyled without touching it. Wynkyn lays the book out in your browser with Paged.js and shows the real pages as you write: the same pagination the printer gets, so there is no surprise at the end.

For printing, it arranges the pages on each sheet so that the folded sheets nest into signatures, the gatherings a book is sewn from, with the pages in reading order. It knows which way your printer turns the paper for two-sided printing, and if your printer prints one side only it gives you the fronts and the backs as separate files to run through twice. A proof mode prints one signature of numbered placeholder pages to fold and check before you commit paper to the whole book. It also writes an EPUB for e-readers, where the text reflows to fit the screen.

Your books are yours

Wynkyn never reads, analyses, trains on, shares or sells your manuscripts, and never will. There is no backend server: the site is a set of plain files your browser downloads once, and the editor, the page layout, the printing and the EPUB export all run on your own machine. Your manuscript is never uploaded anywhere, because there is nowhere for it to go. In Chrome or Edge it opens a folder on your own computer and works on the files there, so a book you make in Wynkyn is a folder you can back up, keep old copies of, or edit with any other tool. Other browsers keep books in browser storage, and you can download a book as a zip at any time.

Built on Paged.js

None of this would be possible without Paged.js, the open-source library that turns HTML into paged, printable books inside a browser. Running heads, folios, footnotes, page breaks that fall where a book needs them: Paged.js does the hard part, and Wynkyn is a small set of tools built on top of it. It is founded by Adam Hyde and maintained by Julien Taquet, Fred Chasen and Gijs de Heij, and it is free to use. If Wynkyn earns a place on your shelf, they are the people to thank. Their GitHub page is where the project takes sponsorship, and the best place to support it.

Free and open source

Wynkyn is MIT licensed. The source is on GitHub, where it can also run as a local app with a command-line export that renders print-ready PDFs. Everything you can do on your own device is free and will stay free.

The name

Wynkyn de Worde came to England from the Rhineland to work for William Caxton, who had brought the first printing press to the country in 1476. When Caxton died in 1492, de Worde took over the press at Westminster and, around 1500, moved it into the City of London, to Fleet Street, where he set up shop at the sign of the Sun. He was the first printer there, and the street stayed the home of English printing and newspapers for the next five centuries.

Caxton had printed for the court and the wealthy. De Worde made printing a trade: around eight hundred editions in his lifetime, many of them small, cheap books of the kind ordinary readers could buy, from grammars and romances to devotional works. He was the first in England to print music from movable type and to use italic type. More than anyone else, he turned the press from a novelty into the way books were made in Britain.

A sun sits in every printer’s device he used. The mark on this site is his sun.
